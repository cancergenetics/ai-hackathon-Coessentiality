DepMap co-essentiality · protein complexes · an AI-agent project

Predicting protein complexes from shared genetic dependencies

What a label-free co-essentiality score for protein complexes does, how a team of AI agents arrived at it, and what was learned along the way.

Built by AI agents (Claude) with minimal human input; no result has yet been checked by a human. Every number comes from a committed result file or from running the code.

In one sentence: the method takes the DepMap CRISPR gene-effect matrix (1,361 cancer cell lines × 18,434 genes) and gives every pair of genes a score that ranks how likely their two proteins are subunits of the same protein complex, without using any training labels.

In brief, how: it cleans each gene's knockout profile, then scores a pair on three views: how similar the two profiles are (F), how much the two genes' genome-wide partner lists overlap (B), and how close the two genes sit in a map of the whole partner network (E). The three are put on a common scale and averaged, which is why the code calls the method zavg_BEF.

What was shown, in brief. On complexes held out from all development work, the method reached a weighted average precision of 0.345, against 0.242 for the best standard method (GLS), 0.146 for Spearman and 0.134 for Pearson correlation. The gain over GLS was +0.103 (95% CI +0.035 to +0.209). Most of the gain comes from large complexes; for small complexes the evidence is mixed. The official hu.MAP test set has not been scored yet.

Background: what the method works with

Gene effects. DepMap knocks out each gene with CRISPR in hundreds of cancer cell lines and measures how much the cells' growth suffers. The released numbers (Chronos gene effects) are about 0 when losing the gene does not matter and about −1 for a typical essential gene. A gene's profile is its row of 1,361 effects, one per cell line. About 3% of values are missing, mostly because some screening libraries lack guides for some genes.

Co-essentiality. Proteins in one complex work together, so losing any subunit tends to hurt the same cell lines. Their profiles therefore look alike. This is the founding observation of the field [Pan 2018; Boyle 2018; Kim 2019; Wainberg 2021].

The task. Given two proteins, score how likely they belong to the same complex. Labels for checking came from hu.MAP 3.0 [Fischer 2025]: 3,314 positive pairs (same complex) and 631,465 negative pairs (proteins known to be in different complexes) over 1,151 proteins. Positives are rare (0.52%), so the natural measure is the area under the precision–recall curve, estimated as average precision (AP). AP asks: if you go down the ranked list, how many of the pairs you meet are true? Random scoring gives AP equal to the positive rate (about 0.005); perfect scoring gives 1.

How it differs from the standard approach

The standard approach scores a pair by how similar the two profiles are.

  • Pearson or Spearman correlation of the two profiles.
  • GLS (generalised least squares; Wainberg et al. 2021) does the same after a correction. Cell lines are not independent samples: lines of the same tissue, or screened in the same batch, share effects across thousands of genes. A plain correlation counts every cell line as a separate piece of evidence and so rewards shared background patterns. GLS estimates the cell-line covariance (how cell lines co-vary across all genes) and "whitens" the profiles with it, so that patterns shared by many genes count less. It is equivalent to a Pearson correlation computed after whitening.

This method changes two things.

  1. It cleans the profiles more carefully before comparing them (Step 1). Every gene is put on the same scale before the cell-line covariance is estimated, the 20 strongest shared patterns are removed, and the whitening is regularised so that it does not amplify noise.
  2. It does not rely on the direct comparison alone (Steps 2–5). It also asks whether the two genes share the same co-dependent partners across the whole genome, and whether they sit close together in a map of the genome-wide co-dependency network. A direct correlation between two subunits is one noisy number. Their partner lists pool evidence from dozens of other genes, so they are harder to fool.

Why this matters. On the held-out complexes, at the point where a quarter of the true pairs have been found, 77% of the method's predictions are correct, against 35% for GLS (both weighted to the natural 0.5% positive rate). For someone choosing candidates to test in the lab, that is the difference between most top hits being real and most being wrong. The method also uses no labels, so it applies the same way to every gene in the genome.

The five steps

Input. The DepMap CRISPRGeneEffect matrix (release 26Q3 as stated by the project's user; the file has no version stamp). Nothing else: no labels, no protein interaction data, no annotation. Genes missing in 10% or more of cell lines (908 protein-coding genes) are still scored, but are left out of every genome-wide estimate below; the 17,525 others are the background genes.

Step 1. Clean the profiles (gives F)

What it does.

  1. Centre each gene's profile on its mean and fill missing values with that mean.
  2. For estimating the covariance only, scale each gene to unit variance (standardise).
  3. Estimate the 1,361 × 1,361 cell-line covariance from the 17,525 background genes.
  4. Split the covariance into independent patterns (principal components, or eigencomponents), ordered by how much of the variation they explain.
  5. Remove the 20 strongest patterns entirely, and whiten the rest: each remaining pattern of strength λ is rescaled by 1/√(λ + 0.1·λ̄), where λ̄ is the average strength. The term 0.1·λ̄ is a ridge: it stops weak patterns, which are mostly noise, from being blown up.
  6. F for a pair is the correlation (cosine) of the two transformed profiles.

Why it is needed. Raw profiles are dominated by patterns shared by thousands of genes: tissue type, screen quality, overall cell fitness. These make unrelated genes look similar. Whitening is GLS's answer, and this step keeps that logic but changes how it is done:

  • Standardising first. This was the single largest change in this step (+0.061 AP). The likely reason, not tested directly: without it, a few hundred strongly essential genes with large effects dominate the covariance estimate, and putting every gene on the same scale lets it describe typical genes.
  • The ridge. Full whitening divides by √λ, which multiplies the weakest, noisiest patterns most (+0.021 AP for the ridge).
  • Removing 20 patterns. This barely changed F itself (+0.004) but gave cleaner neighbour lists for Steps 2–4.

What goes wrong without it. GLS-style whitening scored 0.279 on the development split where these steps were measured; the cleaned F scored 0.365. Without a whitened base, the next steps fail outright: shared-neighbour scores built on raw Pearson correlation were worse than Pearson itself (0.204 vs 0.238), because shared background patterns put the same genes in everyone's partner list.

Shaped by: turning point 1 (whitening planned from the literature), turning point 4 (gene standardisation and the ridge found in the first screen) and turning point 7 (F kept as its own view). Ideas from GLS [Wainberg 2021], component removal [Boyle 2018; Hassan 2023] and regularised covariance [Ledoit & Wolf 2004; Gheorghe & Hart 2022].

Step 2. List each gene's closest partners

What it does. For every gene, compute F against all 17,525 background genes. Convert each gene's similarities to z-scores against that gene's own average and spread, and average the two directions: V(a, b) = ½ [z of b in a's row + z of a in b's row]. Keep each gene's top 44 partners (0.25% of background genes), weighted by V.

Why it is needed. The partner list summarises where a gene sits in the genome-wide dependency network, and it is the raw material for Steps 3 and 4. The per-gene z-scores matter because some genes are similar to almost everything (hubs); without balancing, they would appear in every gene's list.

Where 44 comes from. The setting is a fraction, not a count: K = round(0.25% × number of background genes) = round(0.0025 × 17,525) = 44 (method.py:138). Background genes are those with less than 10% missing values. A DepMap release with a different number of such genes gives a different K. The 0.25% was chosen in the first screen (turning point 4) as the best of the fractions tried, on development data (seed 0, B after Step 3's row z-score):

Fraction of background genesKHT-AP

The optimum is flat: the differences are small next to the fold-to-fold spread (SD about 0.14), so any value from 0.1% to 0.5% would behave much the same. Because the choice was made on development data, it adds a little to the optimism of the development scores.

What goes wrong without it, or with other settings. Keeping more partners made things worse (1% and 2% of genes scored lower still, on a related base): weak partners dilute the signal. Keeping fewer (0.1%) scored best before the row z-score but worse after it: with so few partners, overlaps rest on a handful of genes and become noisy. Unweighted lists (just "in or out") lost 0.04–0.11 AP: the strength of each link carries information.

Shaped by: turning point 4. Per-gene balancing follows mutual-rank and balanced-correlation ideas [Obayashi & Kinoshita 2009; Rohde 2025].

Step 3. Score shared neighbours (gives B)

What it does.

  1. For a pair (a, b), take the two weighted partner lists from Step 2 and compute their overlap as a cosine similarity. Before comparing, remove b from a's list and a from b's, so that only third-party partners count.
  2. Compare this overlap with the overlaps a has with every other gene, and the same for b (a row z-score), and average the two. This is B.

Why it is needed. Subunits of one complex share partners: the other subunits, assembly factors and pathway members. Each shared partner is a separate piece of evidence, so the overlap is more robust than the single direct correlation. Removing the pair itself means B measures something F does not. The row z-score corrects for density: genes inside a large, tightly knit module overlap with all their neighbours, and B should reward overlap that is unusual for these two genes.

What goes wrong without it. This step gave the largest gain of the whole method: +0.036 AP for the overlap and +0.034 more for the row z-score (seed-0 development split). Over five cross-validation repeats, B scored 0.448 against 0.395 for F.

Its weakness. The gain is concentrated in large complexes. Averaged complex by complex (the per-complex AP, or macro AP), B was no better than GLS (0.351 vs 0.349): its overall lead came from the 10 largest clusters of complexes. The likely reason is that small complexes have few partners to share. B also ranks the very top of the list well but the whole list less well (AUROC 0.755 vs 0.765 for GLS). This weakness is why Steps 4 and 5 exist.

Shaped by: turning point 1 (planned), turning point 2 (prioritised), turning point 4 (found to be the largest gain) and turning point 5 (found to help mainly large complexes). The idea of shared neighbours comes from network analysis: shared-nearest-neighbour similarity [Jarvis & Patrick 1973] and topological overlap [Ravasz 2002; Zhang & Horvath 2005].

Step 4. Map the partner network (gives E)

What it does.

  1. Build a network: each gene is linked to its 44 partners from Step 2 (a link is kept if either gene lists the other).
  2. Compute a Laplacian eigenmap [Belkin & Niyogi 2003]: 512 coordinates per gene, chosen so that genes joined by many short paths in the network end up close together. Technically these are the top eigenvectors of the normalised network matrix.
  3. E is the cosine similarity of two genes' coordinates. Genes outside the background set are placed from their own partner lists (Nyström extension).

Why it is needed. E does better on small complexes: on the per-complex average it scored 0.406 against 0.351 for B. The likely reason: B only sees partners shared directly, whereas the map also reflects indirect connections (partners of partners) and smooths over links missing by chance, which matters when a complex's members share only one or two partners.

What goes wrong without it, or with other settings. Without E, the final average loses about 0.012 overall and 0.020 on the per-complex average (0.458 and 0.375 for B + F, against 0.470 and 0.395 with E). Too few dimensions blur complexes together (32 dimensions: 0.321; 128: 0.429; 512: 0.460; 1,024: 0.451).

Cost. E is the slow part: about 100 of the roughly 120–140 seconds the whole method takes on a laptop.

Shaped by: turning point 6. The embedding idea came from the PI agent's brief, not from the literature review.

Step 5. Put the views on one scale and average them

What it does. Draw 200,000 random pairs of background genes once (fixed random seed). For each view, record its mean and standard deviation over these pairs, and convert every score to a z-score with them. The final score is the plain average: (z_B + z_E + z_F) / 3.

Why it is needed. The three views have different scales (a random pair has F of about ±0.03 but B of about ±1), so they must be standardised before averaging. Almost no random pair is a co-complex pair, so random pairs give a label-free reference. Because the reference is fixed, a pair's score never depends on which other pairs are being scored. Equal weights need no labels, and nothing is fitted that could overfit. The views make different mistakes (B is strongest in large complexes, E and F in small ones), so their average is steadier than any one of them.

What goes wrong without it. B alone was a pre-specified backup candidate. On the held-out test it beat GLS overall (0.317 vs 0.242) but fell clearly behind on the per-complex average (0.296 vs 0.369), so it failed the pre-set criterion that the final score passed. Learned weights did not help: logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees over seven views scored 0.462 and 0.466, against 0.470 for the plain average.

Shaped by: turning point 2 (average z-scores, not ranks), turning point 3 (fixed random-pair reference), turning point 5 (greedy ensembles are noise), turning point 7 (learned weights do not help) and turning point 8 (the pre-specified choice and the test).

Output. One number per gene pair, in units of "standard deviations above a random pair", averaged over three views. It is a ranking score, not a probability. Across all 169.8 million pairs of protein-coding genes, 667,677 (0.39%) score 3 or more. Negative scores mean the two genes behave less alike than a random pair.

Worked example: the SMC5/6 complex

The main point: for an easy pair, every method works. For a hard pair, where one gene's own signal is weak, the shared-neighbour step finds what direct correlation misses. For a complex with no growth effect, nothing works.

The example traces two real pairs from the SMC5/6 complex, an 8-protein DNA-repair complex (SMC5, SMC6, NSMCE1–4, SLF1, SLF2), through every step. All numbers come from running the published code on the DepMap file (worked_example.py, output in data/worked_example.json, about 11 minutes). "Rank" means the partner's position among about 18,400 genes in the gene's own ranked list (the better of the two genes' lists); 1 is the top. SMC5/6 is in the development set, so this illustrates the method; it is not evidence of performance.

  • Easy pair: SMC5–SMC6, the two core subunits.
  • Hard pair: NSMCE2–SLF1. SLF1 is a loader protein whose knockout barely affects growth (mean effect −0.11, against −0.50 for NSMCE2), so its profile carries little signal.

1. Clean the profiles (F)

Standardise. Gene spreads differ a lot: the median gene has SD 0.14, the ribosomal gene RPL3 has 0.53. Each gene is scaled to SD 1 before the covariance is estimated.

Covariance and filter. The 1,361 × 1,361 cell-line covariance has 1,360 usable components (one is lost by centring; two more have zero strength). After standardising, the strongest component explains 2.3% of the variation and the top 20 together 17.3%; these 20 are removed. The rest are rescaled with the ridge: component 21 gets weight 0.449 (0.454 without the ridge), component 1,351 gets 2.05 (2.70 without). So the ridge mostly tames the weakest components. Each gene is left with a cleaned profile of 1,340 numbers.

2. Neighbour lists

Each gene keeps its 44 most similar background genes (after the per-gene z-scoring).

  • SMC5's list starts SMC6, NSMCE1, NSMCE2, NSMCE4A, NSMCE3, NFATC2IP, RNF4, SLF2, TRAIP, USP5, NUP62, JCAD, SLF1 (13th), …
  • NSMCE2's list starts SMC5, NSMCE1, SMC6, NSMCE3, NSMCE4A, NFATC2IP, RNF4, SLF2, SAE1, RMI1, … SLF1 is not in it.
  • SLF1's list is noisy, as expected for a weak gene: FGF19, IGLL1, INTS6L, NSMCE3, SMC5, DDX17, … It also contains NSMCE4A, SLF2, SMC6, NSMCE1, TRAIP and NFATC2IP. NSMCE2 is not in it.

So neither gene lists the other, but both list the same complex members.

3. Shared neighbours (B)

Overlap. NSMCE2's and SLF1's lists share 8 genes: SMC5, NSMCE1, SMC6, NSMCE3, NSMCE4A, SLF2, TRAIP and NFATC2IP. Two random 44-gene lists would share 0.11 genes on average. The weighted overlap (cosine) is 0.350.

Per-gene background. A gene's overlaps with all other genes are tiny: for SMC5 they average 0.002 with a spread of 0.018. Against NSMCE2's and SLF1's own backgrounds, an overlap of 0.350 sits about 26 spreads above typical, so B = 25.8. Ranked by B, the pair is now first (against 473rd under GLS).

For the easy pair, SMC5 and SMC6 share 15 partners (B = 43.7). Besides the complex itself, the shared partners include SUMO-pathway genes (SAE1, UBA2, UBE2I, RNF4, NFATC2IP), consistent with NSMCE2 being a SUMO ligase. The method was not told this.

4. Graph embedding (E)

In the 512-dimensional map of the partner network, NSMCE2 and SLF1 have cosine E = 0.681; a random pair has 0.000 ± 0.047. SMC5–SMC6 have 0.978. The map places SLF1 close to the complex even though its own list is noisy, because it is connected to the complex through several partners.

6. The whole complex, a decoy, and a failure

The whole complex. Of the 28 pairs within SMC5/6, the final score puts all 28 in the partner's top 10; Pearson puts 25 in the top 10 and GLS 21 in the top 44. The seven SLF1 pairs are where GLS struggles (ranks 62–473); the final score ranks them 1–7.

A decoy. SMC5's 9th-best Pearson partner is the olfactory-receptor gene OR5M10 (r = 0.247), which has no plausible link to DNA repair. Under the final score it falls to rank 5,148.

A failure. The BBSome is an 8-protein ciliary complex whose genes barely affect growth in culture (BBS1: mean effect −0.12, SD 0.16, a typical non-essential gene). No method puts any of its 28 pairs in the top 44, and BBS1 and BBS2 share no partners at all. When knockouts carry no signal, shared neighbours have nothing to share.

Pseudocode and where each step lives in the code

input: X  (cell lines × genes gene-effect matrix, with missing values)
bg    = genes with < 10% missing values                         # 17,525 genes

# Step 1: clean profiles  ->  F
Xc    = X − gene means;  Xc[missing] = 0
S     = covariance over cell lines of standardise(Xc[:, bg])     # 1,361 × 1,361
U, λ  = eigendecomposition(S), strongest first
w_i   = 0 for i ≤ 20;  1/sqrt(λ_i + 0.1·mean(λ)) otherwise
P     = unit_length_rows( Xc^T · U · diag(w) )                  # one cleaned profile per gene
F(a,b) = P[a] · P[b]

# Step 2: neighbour lists
for each gene a:  z_a = (F(a, bg) − mean) / sd                  # gene a's own background
V(a,b) = (z_a[b] + z_b[a]) / 2
N[a]   = top 44 background genes by V(a, ·), weights max(V, 0)

# Step 3: shared neighbours  ->  B
SO(a,b) = cosine(N[a] without b, N[b] without a)
B(a,b)  = ( rowz_a(SO(a,b)) + rowz_b(SO(a,b)) ) / 2              # z within each gene's row

# Step 4: graph embedding  ->  E
G      = graph linking each gene to N[a] (symmetrised by max)
Emb    = top 512 non-trivial eigenvectors of D^-1/2 G D^-1/2  (Laplacian eigenmap)
E(a,b) = cosine(Emb[a], Emb[b])

# Step 5: combine
for v in {B, E, F}:  μ_v, σ_v = mean, sd of v over 200,000 random background pairs (seed 0)
score(a,b) = mean over v of (v(a,b) − μ_v) / σ_v

Results and how strong the evidence is

The main point: the method clearly beat Pearson, Spearman and GLS on both development data and a held-out test, but the held-out test is small, and the advantage is concentrated in large complexes.

How it was tested

  • Holding out whole complexes. hu.MAP's negatives include nearly every other pair among the same proteins. If the same protein appears in training and test pairs, a method can learn "this protein has many partners" and look better than it is [Park & Marcotte 2012]. So proteins were grouped into clusters (connected groups of positive pairs, with the one giant group of 144 proteins split into 7 communities; 247 clusters in total), and whole clusters were held out.
  • Development set (190 clusters, 2,299 positive and 320,229 negative pairs): five-fold cross-validation grouped by cluster, repeated with five random seeds. Every design choice was made here.
  • Internal final test (57 clusters, 847 positive and 40,786 negative pairs, about 25% of positives): set aside before any method was built, and scored once, with the candidates and pass criterion fixed in advance.
  • Weighting. Holding out clusters keeps too many within-cluster pairs in each test fold. Each pair was weighted by the inverse of its chance of being in the fold (Horvitz–Thompson weighting [Horvitz & Thompson 1952]), so the scored pairs have the natural 0.53% positive rate. All AP values here are this weighted AP (HT-AP).
  • Baselines were given their best variant, chosen on the development data: signed scores (higher = more alike), and GLS without the olfactory-receptor correction, which hurt on this release.

Caveats on the evidence.

  • Development numbers are optimistic. About 300 label-free variants were screened on these data. A nested check of the final choice gave 0.468 against 0.470, but it does not cover the whole search.
  • The held-out set was not perfectly clean. Before it was split off, two reviewer agents computed AP of whitening variants on all pairs, including future test pairs. No choice was made from those numbers, but they set the expectation that whitening helps. This was recorded and disclosed.
  • Held-out scores are lower than development scores for every method (GLS 0.312 → 0.242; final 0.470 → 0.345). The held-out clusters probably happen to be harder; the ranking of methods did not change.
  • The official hu.MAP test set has not been scored. It was withheld from the agents by design.

Limitations, assumptions and when not to use it

Assumptions.

  • Co-complex proteins have similar knockout effects in cancer cell lines. This fails when a complex's loss does not affect growth in culture (for example, complexes acting only in specific tissues), and for complexes whose subunits have opposite effects.
  • Random gene pairs are a fair reference for z-scores (true for the roughly 99.5% of pairs that are not co-complex).
  • The DepMap release behaves like the one used (26Q3, as stated; the file carries no version stamp).

Not validated.

  • The official hu.MAP test pairs, and any external benchmark (for example CORUM).
  • Performance on very large complexes: hu.MAP 3.0 complexes have at most 30 subunits.
  • Calibration: a score of 5 does not correspond to a known probability.
  • Other DepMap data (RNAi screens, other releases), or other organisms.

Known weaknesses.

  • Small complexes. On held-out data, the per-complex average is a tie with GLS (0.365 vs 0.369).
  • Weak signal for non-essential genes. Pairs of two non-essential genes reach only 0.276 AP. Complexes whose loss barely affects growth (for example the BBSome; see the worked example) are not found.
  • Chromosome-position artefact. CRISPR cutting can affect neighbouring genes on the same chromosome arm [Lazar 2024]. In a control on olfactory-receptor genes, which should not be related, 8.4% of pairs scored above the 99th percentile of random pairs (22.6% for pairs on the same chromosome), against 6.6% for GLS. The neighbourhood steps slightly amplify this artefact. Treat high-scoring pairs of nearby genes with caution.
  • Proteins without a usable DepMap profile cannot be scored: immunoglobulin segments, mitochondrially encoded proteins, and multi-copy genes such as histones (about 3.7% of hu.MAP positive pairs). 908 genes with ≥10% missing values are scored but flagged.

When not to use it.

  • To score a pair from two profiles alone: every score depends on the whole genome-wide matrix.
  • As a probability or with a fixed cut-off carried over from another release.
  • To find genetic interactions such as paralog buffering or synthetic lethality: the score was built and tested only for co-complex membership, and negative correlations were found to carry no useful signal for that task.
  • Without checking the gene pair's chromosome positions and essentiality, when deciding on follow-up experiments.

The main point: the final method is the sum of three findings made in about six hours on 27 October 2026.

  1. Clean the profiles before comparing them. Whitening came from the literature (GLS). Standardising genes first was the agents' own addition, and it turned out to be the most useful change to the first-order score.
  2. Ask whether two genes share partners. This was planned from the literature, pushed up the priority list by an early look at the labels that should not have happened, and confirmed as the largest gain in the first screen.
  3. Add a graph embedding and average the views. This came last, after a verification showed that the shared-partner score helped only large complexes, and after every attempt to learn from labels failed.

The work was done by a principal-investigator agent (the "PI", Claude Opus 5.5) and 25 sub-agents it briefed (18 Claude Opus 5.5, 7 Claude Sonnet 5.5). Humans wrote the task prompt, supplied the data and sent one message, "continue", during the research. All times below are from the session record and git commits (UTC, which was also local time).

The setting in one paragraph

The prompt asked for a co-essentiality method that predicts co-complex membership, optimises AUPRC, "clearly beats" Pearson, Spearman and GLS, and uses leakage-aware splits. It required a literature review, a design document reviewed up to three times, a research log and independent verification. The official hu.MAP test set was withheld. Words used below: dev is the development data used for every choice (190 clusters of complexes, cross-validated); the internal final test is the 57 clusters set aside and scored once; macro AP (per-complex AP) is AP averaged complex by complex, so that small complexes count as much as large ones.

1. The starting plan: whitening and shared partners (12:16–12:31)

Situation. Before touching the data, the PI needed a design. It launched a literature agent and an infrastructure agent in parallel.

Options on the table. The literature review (LITERATURE.md) listed 13 ideas. The two that matter here:

  • Whitening by the cell-line covariance, the core of GLS [Wainberg 2021], reported by Gheorghe & Hart (2022) as the largest single gain among normalisation choices.
  • Second-order similarity: compare two genes' whole similarity rows or their shared top neighbours, because "complex members share partners" (LITERATURE.md §6.1, idea 4, rated medium–high benefit).

Decision. The PI's first design put both on the plan: a family of whitening filters that includes GLS as a special case (remove k components, add a ridge), and sparse second-order scores. Ensembles were planned as rank averages. Gene standardisation, graph embeddings and z-score averaging were not in this first plan.

What it produced. The skeleton of Step 1 and the idea behind Step 3.

3. Deciding what "better" means (12:36–13:29)

Situation. hu.MAP negatives cover nearly every other pair among the same proteins, so random pair splits leak protein identity. Positives are also clumped: one connected group of 144 proteins holds about 26% of them.

Options considered.

  • Grouping. Random pairs (leaky), connected components (one giant fold), or components with the giant one split into communities (chosen: 247 clusters).
  • Weighting. Grouping by cluster leaves too many within-cluster pairs in each test fold (positive rate 1.8–9% instead of 0.53%). Round 1 proposed reweighting negatives uniformly. Round 2 pointed out that this still over-weights the hardest negatives (same-cluster pairs) 4–5 times, and proposed Horvitz–Thompson weights (inverse probability of a pair being in the fold). The PI adopted this and sent it to the infrastructure agent while it was still working.
  • The pass rule for the internal test. A power simulation showed the 57-cluster test behaves like about 8 independent clusters; a rule requiring its confidence interval to exclude zero would pass at most 22% of the time even with a true 20% gain. Before any look, the PI moved the interval requirement to the pooled dev predictions and kept a ≥10% gain requirement on the test (design v3.1).

Outcome. The evaluation was frozen before any method was built: HT-weighted AP, five-fold cross-validation grouped by cluster, five seeds, a cluster bootstrap, macro AP and AP without the largest clusters as guard-rails, and two candidates at most for one test look. In hindsight the power amendment did not change the result: the final score's test interval against GLS also excluded zero.

What it produced. No step of the method directly, but the guard-rails exposed the weakness in turning point 5. A round-2 reviewer's point that per-batch standardisation breaks reproducibility led to computing z-score references once, from random genome-wide pairs (Step 5).

Where: DESIGN.md §3 and revision log; docs/reviews/round2_pi_statistics.md; transcript 13:04 ("adopting; forwarding to infra") and 13:29; commits 1155d55, 47dd58b, d94db6f.

4. The first screen: standardise genes, then count shared partners (13:05–14:05)

Situation. A layer-building agent computed 128 label-free scores genome-wide; a screening agent scored them on dev. The PI's brief to the layer builder added one item not found in the literature review or any review: "optional per-gene standardisation".

Evidence (dev, seed 0):

  • Standardising genes before estimating the covariance lifted the whitened scores from about 0.30 to 0.36. After that, results were flat across the number of removed components (0–20) and the ridge strength (0.01–0.3). Unregularised GLS-like whitening was the worst whitened setting (0.279); removing 40 components hurt.
  • The shared-partner score added +0.036, and comparing it against each gene's own background (row z-score) another +0.034. Fewer partners were better, down to about 0.25% of genes.
  • The best layer, which became B, scored 0.435 against 0.288 for GLS.

A caveat the record makes clear. B was not in the first, pre-planned grid: the standardised base and the 0.25% setting came from a second, dev-guided round. This is one reason dev numbers are optimistic. Also, the 20 removed components that the method keeps barely matter for the first-order score (+0.004); they help B slightly (0.401 vs 0.389).

Decision. "Key finding … Needs independent verification before I rely on it." The PI launched an independent re-implementation and, at the same moment, a graph-methods agent as the "natural next step from second-order success".

What it produced. Step 1 as it stands (standardise, ridge), Step 2 and Step 3.

Where: results/phaseA/README.md; results/layers/screen_v2.manifest.json; transcript 13:05 and 14:05; commits 920b8c0, 34be32c, 7bd4c4b.

5. The ensemble fails and the gain turns out to be lopsided (13:49–14:13)

Situation. The design said a z-averaged ensemble should be the default final candidate. The screening agent built one by greedy forward selection.

Evidence.

  • The ensemble was selection noise. Greedy selection added to B a layer that scored only 0.250 on its own. In nested cross-validation (choose on four folds, test on the fifth) the ensemble scored 0.428, exactly the same as the best single layer. Nearly 300 of the project's 822 trials went into this.
  • B's gain was concentrated. The verifier, asked explicitly whether the gain came from a few clusters, reproduced B exactly from the raw file and found that the 10 largest clusters held 64% of the positive pairs scored. Without them, B's gain over GLS fell from +0.155 to +0.039 (interval including zero), and its macro AP equalled GLS (0.326 vs 0.325).

Decision. B became the provisional best, and "gain comes from a few clusters" became a standard check for every later headline result. The PI's response to the small-complex weakness was a time-boxed supervised attempt "targeting smaller complexes" (turning point 7). The graph work that eventually helped small complexes had already been launched six minutes earlier for a different reason.

What it produced. The reason for Step 4 and Step 5 to exist, and the lesson that ensemble members must be pre-specified, not greedily picked.

Where: results/phaseA/ENSEMBLE.md; results/verify/README.md; transcript 14:13; commit a2f8622.

6. A graph embedding sees what shared partners miss (14:06–15:30)

Situation. The graph agent tried 69 ways to go beyond direct partner overlap on the same neighbour network: topological overlap, soft thresholds, diffusion (random walks, heat kernel) and spectral embeddings. The embedding idea came from the PI's brief, not from the literature review or a reviewer.

Evidence.

  • The 512-dimensional Laplacian eigenmap (E) scored 0.463 (5 seeds) against 0.448 for B, but the difference's interval included zero, so by the pre-set rule it could not replace B.
  • Its macro AP was clearly higher: 0.394 against 0.341 on seed 0 (0.406 vs 0.351 over five seeds). It did better on small complexes.
  • Dimensions mattered (32: 0.321; 128: 0.429; 512: 0.460; 1,024: 0.451); topological overlap and long random walks did not help.

Decision. "Complementary view": E was kept as a candidate member of an average rather than as a replacement.

What it produced. Step 4.

Where: results/phaseA_graph/README.md; transcript 15:30; commits 3bd38a6, ea7c7f5.

7. Learning from labels fails three times, and brings back the simple score (13:34–16:18)

Situation. The prompt invited any method, and the design planned supervised models as the main route. Three families were tried:

  • Stacking v1 (13:34–14:09): gradient-boosted trees and logistic regression over 12 pre-chosen similarity scores, with gene-level features. Trees 0.383 vs a plain average of the same scores 0.357; the difference +0.006 had an interval spanning zero.
  • Metric learning (14:13–15:42): learn cell-line weights or low-dimensional projections from labelled pairs. Every version stayed within ±0.003 of its starting point. Training loss fell from 0.57 to 0.09 while validation AP did not move: the models memorised about 650 training genes.
  • Stacking v2 (15:46–16:18): logistic regression and trees over seven frozen views. 0.462 and 0.466, against 0.470 for the plain average; worse or equal in 72 of 80 seed-by-model comparisons.

The useful side finding. Metric learning was started to help small complexes. It did not, but its baseline row showed that the plain first-order score F matched B outside the 10 largest clusters and had higher macro AP (0.371 vs 0.341). The PI recorded this as "complementary to second-order".

What it taught. With about 2,000 training positives per fold in about 150 clusters, labels did not transfer to unseen complexes beyond what label-free geometry already captured. Gene-level features carried only a weak, general signal (essential genes are more often in complexes).

What it produced. The decision that the final method is label-free with equal weights (Step 5), and F's place in it (Step 1).

Where: results/phaseB/, results/phaseC/README.md, results/phaseB_v2/README.md; transcript 15:42; commits b3480c2, 26623ab, e242a7c.

8. A rule written before the result, and one look at the test (15:42–17:36)

Situation. By 15:42 there were three views with different strengths: B (best overall), E and F (better on small complexes). The original idea of a z-averaged ensemble, which had failed when its members were redundant, now had complementary members.

Options considered. The PI wrote four equal-weight averages by hand (B+E, B+F, B+E+F, B+E+F+heat diffusion) alongside the learned stackers, and before running them wrote decision rule D3: the best combination replaces B as the primary test candidate only if its gain over B has a confidence interval above zero in at least 3 of 5 seeds and its macro AP is not lower; otherwise B is primary.

Evidence.

  • B+E+F scored 0.470 (macro 0.395) against B's 0.448 (0.351); its gain over B had an interval above zero in all five seeds. B+E+F+diffusion tied (0.469) at twice the runtime.
  • Nested selection among the four averages gave 0.468, so choosing among them added little optimism.
  • A second verifier rebuilt everything, including the eigenmap, independently and matched to 2.6e-5. It also found the margin over B fragile: with 256 dimensions instead of 512, the interval stayed above zero in only 2 of 5 seeds.
  • The one look (17:35), with the command fixed in advance: final score 0.345, B 0.317, GLS 0.242, Spearman 0.146, Pearson 0.134. The final score passed against all three baselines. B, the backup, would have failed: its macro AP was 0.296 against 0.369 for GLS. A third verifier re-derived every test number with its own code.

What it produced. The final method (Step 5). The test showed that combining views protected against B's small-complex weakness: the final score tied GLS on macro AP (0.365 vs 0.369), where B fell far behind. It did not show that the method beats GLS on small complexes.

Where: RESEARCH_TREE.md decisions D3–D4; results/phaseB_v2/decision.json; results/ift/look01_2026-10-27.json; commits e884f44, e242a7c, 460fd36, 8267142, b914a8e.

The research process

Who did what.

  • The PI agent wrote the design and all decision notes, briefed every sub-agent, forwarded reviewers' changes to running agents, fixed one reproducibility bug itself, chose the combinations to test, wrote the pre-specified rule and ran the single test look. It read sub-agents' summaries, not their raw logs.
  • Sub-agents wrote all the code and ran all the experiments: literature (1), infrastructure (2), design reviewers (8), method scientists (6), engineering (3), independent verifiers (3) and retrospectives (2).
  • Humans gave the prompt and the data, and typed "continue" once (15:28). That message ended a 17-minute pause in which the PI was waiting with no work blocked; the next result arrived two minutes later, so it did not change the method. A few questions after the final report (for example, what the method is called) had no effect on it.

Where exploration was productive.

  • Computing all label-free scores genome-wide once and caching them made each screen fast (148 scores evaluated in about 4 minutes).
  • Independent verifiers changed conclusions twice: they exposed B's concentration in large clusters, and the fragility of the final score's margin over B.
  • Writing the decision rule before running the final combination round, and allowing one test look, kept the end-game clean.

Where it went in circles.

  • Supervised learning was run to completion three times after the first null result. Metric learning was null by about 14:46 but ran its full ladder until 15:42. It ran in parallel with the graph work, so it cost little time on the critical path, and it produced the F side finding.
  • A greedy ensemble used about 300 of 822 trials for a null result.
  • Idle waiting. 104 of the PI's 175 messages in the research session were one-word "Waiting." replies to progress notifications. The largest stall was about 40 minutes when the final-evaluation agent's test suite hung (two numerical libraries deadlocked in one process); the PI waited from 16:41 to 17:35.
  • Concurrency side effects. Agents working in the same folder left untracked files that defeated the "refuse to run on uncommitted code" guard; several runs used the override flag (recorded in their run records).

Where human input changed direction. During the research, it did not. The direction changes all came from agents: the round-2 statistics reviewer (weighting), the power simulation (pass rule), the verifier (concentration in large clusters) and the graph agent (complementary embedding). After the research, humans asked for the manuscript and the public repository; that phase added post-hoc analyses (a component-removed Pearson reference and controls on the final score) but did not change the method.

The main point: the biology lesson is that genes in the same complex are recognised more reliably by who else they depend on together with than by how closely their own profiles match, at least for large complexes. The method lesson is that, with a few thousand labelled pairs, careful label-free geometry beat every supervised model the agents tried, and that pooled benchmark numbers hide where a gain comes from.

Insights are sorted into three groups and ranked within each by how general and how surprising they are. Each links to where it arose. "Shown" means measured in this project; "believed" means a likely explanation that was not tested.

1. Scientific and domain insights

These would interest a researcher working with DepMap or protein complexes, whatever method they use.

S1. Shared co-dependency partners carry more co-complex signal than direct profile similarity, but mainly for large complexes. Shown. Scoring a pair by the overlap of the two genes' top-44 partner lists raised development AP from 0.395 (direct similarity of the same cleaned profiles) to 0.448. The gain came almost entirely from the ten largest complex clusters: averaged complex by complex, the overlap score tied GLS (0.351 vs 0.349). Believed: small complexes have too few partners to share. → Turning points 4 and 5; Step 3.

S2. How the cell-line covariance is estimated matters more than how many components are removed. Shown. Standardising each gene before estimating the covariance added +0.061 AP, the largest single change to the first-order score. After it, removing 0, 5 or 20 components and ridge strengths from 0.01 to 0.3 all gave 0.359–0.365. Unregularised whitening, as in GLS, was the worst whitened setting. Believed, not tested: without standardisation, a few hundred strongly essential genes with large variance dominate the covariance. → Turning point 4; Step 1.

S3. A few thousand labelled pairs did not improve on label-free scores for unseen complexes. Shown. With about 1,800–2,000 training positives per fold in about 150 clusters, logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees over seven similarity views (0.462, 0.466), adding each gene's essentiality statistics (no gain), and learned cell-line weights or projections (within ±0.003 of their starting point) never beat the plain average (0.470). The learned models fitted the training genes but not new complexes. → Turning point 7.

S4. Pooled precision–recall on co-complex benchmarks is dominated by a handful of large complexes. Shown. The ten largest of 190 development clusters held 64% of out-of-fold positives. The neighbour-overlap score was +0.155 AP over GLS in pooled terms but +0.039 (not significant) without those ten clusters. This confirms, on DepMap and hu.MAP, the warning of Rahman et al. (2021) for CORUM. → Turning point 5.

S5. Anti-correlation carries no useful co-complex signal on balance. Shown. For Pearson, Spearman and GLS alike, signed scores beat absolute values (for example GLS signed 0.255 vs two-sided 0.241 on all pairs). The olfactory-receptor correction of Boyle et al. also lowered GLS on this release. → Turning point 3; appendix.

S6. Neighbourhood scores slightly amplify the CRISPR chromosome-position artefact. Shown. Among unrelated olfactory-receptor gene pairs, 8.4% scored above the 99th percentile of random pairs under the final score (22.6% for same-chromosome pairs), against 6.6% under GLS (expected: 1%). Removing same-chromosome pairs from the benchmark did not lower AP, so the artefact does not drive the benchmark result. → Limitations.

S7. Essential genes are more often in complexes, which any benchmark must account for. Shown. A model that sees only each gene's own essentiality statistics, and no pair similarity, reached 2.6 times the positive rate on unseen complexes. Labelled genes are 31% common-essential against 9% genome-wide. Results should therefore be reported by essentiality group, as here: the final score led GLS in all three groups. → Turning point 7.

2. Methodological insights

These generalise to other pair-prediction or similarity-scoring methods.

M1. When negatives are "all other pairs among the same proteins", hold out whole complexes, then reweight. Shown. Random pair splits let a model learn which proteins have many partners. Holding out clusters of complexes fixes that but changes the mix of pairs in each test fold: too many within-cluster pairs, so too many positives and too many hard negatives (uniform reweighting over-weighted hard negatives 4–5×). Weighting each pair by the inverse of its chance of being in the fold (Horvitz–Thompson) restores the natural 0.53% positive rate. → Turning point 3.

M2. Simulate the power of a held-out test before fixing its pass rule. Shown. The held-out test had 57 clusters but an effective size of about 8 independent clusters. A confidence-interval rule on it would have passed with probability at most 0.22 even if the true gain were 20%. The rule was changed before the test was opened. → Turning point 3.

M3. Combine label-free views by z-scoring against random pairs and averaging with equal weights; distrust greedy ensembles. Shown. A greedy forward-selected ensemble gained in-sample and nothing under nested cross-validation (0.428 vs 0.428 for the best single view). A small set of pre-specified equal-weight averages lost almost nothing under nesting (0.468 vs 0.470). → Turning points 5 and 8; Step 5.

M4. Report every headline gain with its per-cluster average, the gain without the largest clusters, several split seeds and a nested check. Shown. Each of these changed a conclusion here: the per-cluster average exposed B's weakness, seeds showed which differences were stable, and nesting removed the greedy-ensemble gain. → Turning point 5.

M5. Have a separate agent re-implement each headline result from raw data, and vary the chosen settings slightly. Shown. All three verifications reproduced the numbers exactly. Varying the embedding settings showed that the final score's small margin over B was stable in size but not in significance (confidence interval above zero in 5 of 5 seeds at 512 dimensions, 2 of 5 at 256). → Turning points 5 and 8.

M6. Pilot a new supervised approach on one fold against its starting point before running it in full. Shown. Three supervised phases each gave the same null answer only after full five-seed runs. A one-fold pilot with a stop rule (|gain| < 0.005) would have saved most of that compute. → Turning point 7.

M7. Agents that do not evaluate must not see labels. Shown. Two design reviewers, asked only to comment, computed AP on all labelled pairs, including pairs later held out for the final test. Nothing was chosen from those numbers, but the test was no longer fully untouched. Briefs now say "compute no AP or AUROC". → Turning point 2; the research process.

3. Engineering details for reproduction

Needed only to rerun or port the code.

  • Community detection must be seeded and hash-stable. Louvain tie-breaking depended on Python's string hashing, so the cluster assignment changed between runs. All results use PYTHONHASHSEED=0; the assignment is committed as results/splits/protein_assignment.csv. (The research process)
  • The covariance is rank-deficient in this DepMap release (rank 1,357 of 1,361); GLS drops 4 dependent cell lines found by pivoted QR. The method's ridge term makes this harmless.
  • Remove the intercept exactly by projecting onto an orthonormal basis of the complement of the all-ones vector (Householder), rather than re-centring cell lines.
  • The eigenmap is seeded (ARPACK start vector from seed 0) and built from a float32 normalised adjacency, as in the original run; results agree to about 1e-5 across reimplementations. The spectrum is nearly degenerate around 512 dimensions, so small numerical changes can rotate coordinates without changing cosines much.
  • Genes with ≥10% missing values are embedded by a Nyström extension and flagged; they never enter the covariance, the null pairs or other genes' partner lists.
  • Genome-wide row statistics are computed in fixed gene blocks so that each pair's score is identical whatever pairs are requested and in whatever order (tested).
  • torch and LightGBM in one pytest process can deadlock on OpenMP; run those test files separately.
  • Apple Silicon: the default Anaconda was x86 under Rosetta; a native arm64 Python 3.12 environment was needed.
  • Storage: all-pairs z-scores stored as float16 lose precision above |z| ≈ 32 (errors up to 0.016); the ranked pair table stores float32.
  • Runtime: about 2 minutes and 4–7 GB of memory for the full method on an 8-core laptop; the eigenmap takes most of it.
